# Tuscan Local Species Catalogue
## Heritage Varieties for Permaculture — 1 ha, 300m, USDA Zone 8b–9a, Csa Mediterranean

> **Context:** 1 hectare in Tuscany, ~300m altitude, hilly terrain, silty-sandy fertile soil, good water availability. All species listed are documented as locally grown in Tuscany or historically cultivated in the Italian peninsula with specific Tuscan relevance.

# 1. FRUIT TREES

## 1.1 Olive (Olea europaea) — *Olivo*

### Tuscan Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Characteristics | Notes |
|---------|---------------|-------|
| **Frantoio** | The backbone of Tuscan olive oil. Medium vigour, self-fertile but benefits from cross-pollination. Green, herbaceous, peppery oil. | Most widely planted in Tuscany (Chianti, Valdelsa). Rustic, adapts well to hills. |
| **Leccino** | Second most common Tuscan variety. Delicate, balanced flavour. More cold-tolerant than Frantoio. | Good polliniser for other varieties. Widely planted across Tuscany. |
| **Moraiolo** | Small, highly aromatic fruit. Very frost-resistant (survived 2023 freezes better than Frantoio). Intense peppery notes. | Classic Tuscan mountain variety. Vigorous, late ripening. |
| **Pendolino** | Used primarily as a polliniser. Sweet, mild oil. | Pendulous growth habit. Excellent companion planting for Frantoio and Leccino. |
| **Maurino** | Robust, disease-resistant. Often included in "Tuscan blend" oils. | Good polliniser for other Tuscan varieties. |
| **Piangente** | Local Chianti variety. Produces well even in poor soils. | Less common but documented in traditional Tuscan groves. |
| **Raggia** | Traditional Tuscan variety, especially in the Lucca area. | Medium vigour, good oil quality. |

### Additional Italian Varieties (Beyond Tuscany)
> Regional varieties documented for cross-reference and broader Italian agroforestry planning.
| Variety | Region | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Taggiasca** | Liguria | The classic Ligurian olive. Small, delicate oil. Used for taggiasca table olives as well as oil. Traditional in western Liguria. |
| **Coratina** | Puglia | The dominant Apulian variety. Very high polyphenol content — bitter, peppery, robust oil. Extremely productive. Less cold-hardy than Tuscan varieties. |

### Rootstock Preferences
- **Franco (seedling):** Vigorous, drought-tolerant, long-lived. Best for hillside, low-input systems.
- **Leccino seedling:** Common rootstock for Tuscan conditions.
- **Don Carlo®:** Semi-dwarfing, good for denser planting.

### Pollination
- Frantoio and Moraiolo are partially self-fertile but yield significantly better with cross-pollination.
- **Recommended polliniser mix:** Plant 80% Frantoio + 10% Leccino + 10% Pendolino/Maurino for optimal cross-pollination.

### Frost Risk
- Leccino: hardy to -10°C
- Moraiolo: hardy to -12°C (most frost-resistant)
- Frantoio: hardy to -8°C (susceptible to spring frosts)
- At 300m, choose north-facing slopes for Moraiolo, south-facing for Frantoio.

### Spacing
- Traditional: 6–7m × 6–7m (200–280 trees/ha)
- High-density (modern): 5m × 5m (400 trees/ha)
- Permaculture polyculture: 7m × 8m (allow space for understory)

## 1.2 Fig (Ficus carica) — *Fico*

### Tuscan Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Skin Colour | Harvest | Notes |
|---------|------------|---------|-------|
| **Fico Brogiotto Bianco** | Green-yellow | Late summer (breba) + September (main) | "King of figs." Very hardy, vigorous, productive. Red flesh, very sweet. |
| **Fico Brogiotto Nero** | Purple-black | August–September | Similar to Bianco but darker. Rich, honeyed flavour. |
| **Fico Dottato (Bianco del Cilento DOP)** | Greenish-yellow | August–September | Few seeds, excellent for drying. DOP recognised. The classic "Italian fig." |
| **Fico Bianco** | White-green | July–August | Sweet, delicate flavour. Fresh eating. |
| **Fico Paradiso** | Light green | July–September | Local Tuscan variety. Very sweet. |
| **Fico Verdino (Fico Verde)** | Green | August–September | Small to medium fruit, excellent flavour. |

### Additional Italian Varieties
| Variety | Type | Notes |
|---------|------|-------|
| **Dottato** | Commercial dried | Dominates commercial dried-fig production in Italy. Also listed above as DOP variety. The benchmark for dried figs. |
| **Petrelli** | Large early breba | Notable for large early-season breba (first) crop. Important in southern Italian fig culture. |

### Rootstock
- Figs are grown on their own roots (cuttings). No grafting needed.
- Tolerance: drought-tolerant once established, but fruit quality improves with irrigation.

### Pollination
- Common figs (Brogiotto Bianco/Nero, Dottato) are **parthenocarpic** — no pollination needed.
- Will produce a breba (first) crop on old wood, and main crop on new wood.

### Frost Risk
- Young trees: sensitive below -5°C
- Established trees: hardy to -10°C
- At 300m, plant in sheltered, south-facing positions. Mulch base heavily in winter.

### Spacing
- Standard: 5m × 5m (400 trees/ha)
- Permaculture: 6m × 6m

## 1.3 Apple (Malus domestica) — *Melo*

### Tuscan Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Region | Ripening | Use | Notes |
|---------|--------|----------|-----|-------|
| **Mela Casciana** | Garfagnana (Lucca) | August–October | Eating, cooking | Round, slightly flattened, red with green-yellow stripes. Very fragrant, sweet-acid, compact not mealy. Listed in Vetrina Toscana. |
| **Mela Rotella** | Lunigiana (Massa-Carrara) | September–October | Eating, preserves | Ancient cultivar. Round, green with red stripes turning yellow-red. Sweet, aromatic. |
| **Mela Carla** | Tuscany/Italy-wide | September–October | Eating, cooking | Old Italian variety. Yellow-green with red blush. Sweet, aromatic. |
| **Mela Fiorentina** | Florence area | October–November | Eating, storage | Traditional Tuscan variety. |
| **Mela Giovannina** | Mugello | July–August | Early eating | Tuscan summer apple. |
| **Mela Paradisa** | Tuscany | September | Eating | Small, sweet, very aromatic. |
| **Mela Zeraiolo** | Garfagnana | October–November | Storage | Traditional Tuscan winter apple. |
| **Mela della Nonna** | Tuscany | October | Cooking, baking | Heritage variety. |
| **Mela Limoncella** | Central Italy | October–November | Eating, storage | Ancient Italian variety, forms a large tree. |

### Rootstock Preferences
| Rootstock | Vigour | Spacing | Notes for Tuscany |
|-----------|--------|---------|-------------------|
| **M9** | Dwarf (30–40%) | 2.5m × 3m | Needs staking. Good for small spaces, but less drought-tolerant. |
| **M26** | Semi-dwarf (50%) | 3m × 4m | Good compromise. Better drought tolerance than M9. |
| **MM106** | Semi-vigorous (65%) | 4m × 5m | Recommended for Tuscan hillside. Good anchorage, drought-tolerant. |
| **Franco (seedling)** | Vigorous (100%) | 5m × 6m | Best for permaculture. Long-lived, deep-rooted. |

### Pollination
- Most apples are self-sterile. Need a compatible polliniser from the same or overlapping flowering group.
- **Flowering groups (for Tuscany):** Most Tuscan varieties flower in mid-season.
- **Recommended pollinisers:** Plant at least 2–3 varieties. Mela Casciana + Mela Rotella + Mela Fiorentina will cross-pollinate.

### Frost Risk
- Bloom: March–April (risk of late frost at 300m)
- Hardy to -25°C dormant, but blossoms damaged at -2°C
- **Low frost risk** for mid-season varieties at 300m if not in frost pocket.

### Spacing
- Dwarf (M9): 2.5m × 3m
- Semi-dwarf (MM106): 4m × 5m
- Permaculture (seedling): 6m × 6m

### Rootstock
| Rootstock | Vigour | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Cotogno (Cydonia oblonga) — Quince rootstock** | Dwarf (40–50%) | Most common. Use for low-spur systems. Needs good soil. |
| **Cotogno BA29** | Semi-dwarf (60%) | Most vigorous quince rootstock. Better for poorer soils. Better anchorage. |

- Most pears are self-sterile. Exception: **Conference** is partially self-fertile.

### Frost Risk
- Bloom: March–April (similar risk to apple)
- Hardy to -25°C dormant
- Blossom damage at -2°C
- Williams is earlier blooming — higher frost risk at 300m.

### Spacing
- Quince rootstock: 3m × 4m
- Franco (seedling): 5m × 6m
- Permaculture: 6m × 7m

### Rootstock
| Rootstock | Vigour | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **San Giuliano (Franco susino)** | Vigorous (100%) | Seedling rootstock. Best for hillside, long life. |
| **GF 8/1** | Semi-vigorous (70%) | Clonal. Good for medium soils. Better uniformity. |

### Pollination
- Regina Claudia Verde is **self-fertile**.

### Frost Risk
- Bloom: March–April
- Moderate frost risk. Hardy to -25°C dormant.
- Susino di Zucchero blooms slightly later — lower risk.

### Spacing
- San Giuliano rootstock: 5m × 5m
- Permaculture: 5m × 6m

### Tuscan/Italian Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Region | Ripening | Notes |
|---------|--------|----------|-------|
| **Ciliegia di Lari IGP** | Pisa area, Tuscany | May–June | **The flagship Tuscan cherry.** PGI-recognised. Sweet, intense flavour. Grown in Pisa countryside. Celebrated at annual Lari cherry festival. Grown on silty-clay soils. |
| **Ciliegia Durone di Vignola** | Emilia-Romagna (also grown in Tuscany) | May–June | Large, dark red, firm, very sweet. One of Italy's most famous cherry varieties. |
| **Ciliegia Ferrovia** | Puglia (also grown in central Italy) | Late May–June | Large, heart-shaped, dark red. Excellent flavour. |
| **Ciliegia Mora di Vignola** | Emilia/central Italy | June | Dark purple, almost black. Very sweet. |
| **Ciliegia Anella di Bari** | Southern Italy | May | Early variety. |
| **Ciliegia Bigarreau** | Italy | May–June | Firm, pale yellow from Italy. |
| **Ciliegia del Monte** | Tuscan Apennines | June–July | Mountain cherry, small but very flavourful. |

### Rootstock
| Rootstock | Vigour | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Colt (Coltina)** | Semi-vigorous (70%) | Widely used. Adapts to many soils. Good for Tuscany. |
| **Gisela 6** | Semi-dwarf (50–60%) | Precocious, good yields. Needs good soil. |
| **Gisela 5** | Dwarf (40%) | For high-density. Needs irrigation and staking. |
| **Franco (Prunus avium seedling)** | Vigorous (100%) | Long-lived, deep-rooted. Best for permaculture hillside. |

### Pollination
- **Most sweet cherries are self-sterile.** Need cross-pollination.
- **Durone di Vignola** needs a polliniser (e.g., Ferrovia, Bigarreau).
- **Ciliegia di Lari** needs a polliniser.
- Plant at least 2–3 compatible varieties.

### Frost Risk
- **HIGH FROST RISK** at 300m. Cherry blossoms are among the earliest (March–April).
- Flowers damaged at -2°C.
- Choose late-blooming varieties (e.g., Durone) for higher altitude.
- Avoid frost pockets.

### Spacing
- Gisela 6: 4m × 4m
- Colt: 4.5m × 5m
- Franco/Mazzard: 5m × 6m

## 1.9 Persimmon (Diospyros kaki) — *Cachi / Kaki*

### Tuscan/Italian Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Type | Notes |
|---------|------|-------|
| **Kaki Tipo** | Astringent (soft-fleshed) | The classic Italian persimmon. Round, orange-red. Introduced to Italy in late 19th century. Most widely grown in Italy. Ripens to jelly-like softness. |
| **Kaki Vaniglia (Kaki Dolce)** | Non-astringent | Can be eaten when firm. Sweet, crisp. |
| **Rojo Brillante** | Non-astringent | Spanish variety also grown in Italy. Excellent for fresh market. |
| **Kaki Loto (Diospyros lotus)** | Rootstock/fruit | Small, used as rootstock for D. kaki. |

### Rootstock
- **Diospyros lotus (Loto):** Most common. Good vigour, cold-hardy. Tolerates poor soils.
- **Diospyros virginiana:** American persimmon rootstock. More cold-hardy, slower growth.

### Pollination
- **Kaki Tipo** is parthenocarpic (produces seedless fruit without pollination).
- Some varieties need pollination for good fruit set.

### Frost Risk
- **LOW-MODERATE.** Persimmons are dormant in winter. Hardy to -15°C.
- Late frosts can damage new growth and flowers (May).
- At 300m, generally safe.

### Spacing
- Kaki Tipo: 5m × 5m
- Permaculture: 6m × 6m

## 1.11 Chestnut (Castanea sativa) — *Castagno*

### Tuscan Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Region | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Marrone del Mugello IGP** | Mugello (Florence) | The most prestigious Tuscan chestnut. PGI recognised. Large, very sweet, with hints of vanilla and hazelnut. |
| **Castagna del Monte Amiata IGP** | Monte Amiata (Siena/Grosseto) | PGI recognised. Varieties: Cecio, Marrone, and Bastarda Rossa. Sweet, floury texture. |
| **Marrone di Caprese Michelangelo DOP** | Caprese Michelangelo (Arezzo) | DOP recognised. Grown since the 9th century. Known for sweetness and flavour. |
| **Farina di Neccio della Garfagnana DOP** | Garfagnana (Lucca) | DOP flour from local chestnuts. |
| **Castagna della Valle del Reno** | Pistoia Apennines | Local Tuscan chestnut. |
| **Marrone della Valle di Susa** | Piedmont (also grown in Tuscany) | The "king" of marroni. |

### Rootstock
- **Grafted on Castanea sativa seedling** (Franco). Own-root seedlings are also common.
- Chestnuts must be grafted on compatible chestnut rootstock — cannot be grafted on oak.

### Pollination
- **Mostly self-sterile.** Need 2+ different varieties for good nut set.
- Marrone varieties are partially self-fertile but yield better with cross-pollination.
- **Good pairs:** Marrone del Mugello + Castagna del Monte Amiata.

### Frost Risk
- **LOW.** Blooms late (June–July). Hardy to -20°C dormant.
- At 300m, ideal for Tuscan chestnut cultivation.

### Spacing
- Traditional: 10m × 10m (100 trees/ha)
- Modern: 8m × 8m (150 trees/ha)
- Permaculture: 10m × 12m (allow room for understory)

## 1.13 Pistachio (Pistacia vera) — *Pistacchio*

### Italian Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Region | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Pistacchio Verde di Bronte DOP** | Bronte, Sicily (Etna area) | The flagship Italian pistachio. DOP recognised. Small, vibrant green, intensely flavoured. Grown on volcanic soils of Mount Etna. Biennial bearing — heavy crop every other year. |
| **Pistacchio di Raffadali DOP** | Raffadali, Sicily | DOP recognised. Larger than Bronte, also excellent quality. |

### Rootstock
| Rootstock | Notes |
|-----------|-------|
| **Terebinth (Pistacia terebinthus)** | Wild pistachio rootstock. Most common in Italy. Drought-tolerant, adapts to calcareous, poor soils. Excellent for dry hillsides. |
| **Pistacia atlantica** | Also used, more vigorous. |

### Pollination
- **Dioecious** — separate male and female trees.
- Plant 1 male per 8–10 female trees.
- Male trees must be compatible with the female cultivar.

### Frost Risk
- **MODERATE-HIGH.** Hardy to -10°C dormant. Spring frosts can damage flowers.
- At 300m, choose sheltered, south-facing slope. Bronte is hardy enough for Tuscan hills.
- Needs a long, hot summer for nut maturation.

### Spacing
- Standard: 6m × 6m (280 trees/ha)
- Permaculture: 7m × 7m

# 2. BERRY BUSHES & HEDGEROW SPECIES

## 2.1 Elderberry (Sambucus nigra) — *Sambuco*

### Italian Context
- **Sambuco nero (Sambucus nigra)** is native to Italy, including Tuscany.
- Wild elderberry is abundant in hedgerows, forest edges, and abandoned fields throughout Tuscany.
- **Italian varieties:** No specific named Tuscan cultivars are widely commercialised. Use local wild seedstock or the European standard varieties.
- Good named varieties for cultivation: **'Haschberg'** (Austrian, widely grown in Europe), **'Sampo'**, **'Samdal'**, **'Sambu'**.
- **Tuscan culinary use:** Fiori di sambuco (elderflower) used for frittelle, sciroppo, and infusions. Berries used for jams, cordials, and vin di sambuco (elderberry wine).

### Cultivation
- **Spacing:** 3m × 3m
- **Pollination:** Self-fertile (most cultivars)
- **Frost risk:** Very low. Hardy to -25°C.
- **Soil:** Tolerates most soils, thrives in moist conditions. Good for water-rich areas on the 1ha.

## 2.12 Nitrogen-Fixing Hedges Native to Tuscany

### Spanish Broom (Spartium junceum) — *Ginestra di Spagna*

## 3.1 Tomatoes (*Pomodoro* — Solanum lycopersicum)

### Tuscan/Italian Heritage Varieties
| Variety | Type | Use | Season | Notes |
|---------|------|-----|--------|-------|
| **San Marzano DOP** | Plum/elongated | Sauce, canning, passata | Mid-season (70–80 days) | The Italian sauce tomato. Thin skin, few seeds, sweet, low acidity. DOP recognised. Needs staking. |
| **Costoluto Fiorentino** | Ribbed, flattened | Fresh eating, cooking | Mid-season (80 days) | Classic Tuscan tomato. Distinctive deep ribbing. Very fragrant, intense flavour. 150g fruits. |
| **Cuor di Bue (Oxheart)** | Large, heart-shaped | Fresh (slicing) | Mid-late (85 days) | Huge, meaty, pinkish-red. Thick flesh, very few seeds. Sweet, intense tomato flavour. Vigorous plant. |
| **Pomodoro Ciliegia (Cherry)** | Small, round | Fresh, salads | Early (60–70 days) | Cherry tomato — originally Sicilian but widely grown in Tuscany. |
| **Pomodoro Pendolino** | Small, hanging cluster | Fresh, drying | Mid-season | Traditional Tuscan hanging tomato. |

### Planting Times (Tuscany, 300m)
- **Sow indoors:** February–March (in seed trays, protected)
- **Transplant outdoors:** April–May (after last frost — typically mid-April at 300m)
- **Harvest:** July–October

### Frost Tolerance
- **Highly frost-sensitive.** Temperature <10°C stunts growth. <0°C kills plants.
- Plant after all frost risk passes (mid-April to early May at 300m).

### Tuscan Culinary Use
- **San Marzano:** Passata (the base of Tuscan pasta sauces), pizza sauce.
- **Costoluto Fiorentino:** Panzanella (bread salad), fresh in insalata caprese, sugo.
- **Cuor di Bue:** Fresh slices with basil and mozzarella. Pappa al pomodoro (Tuscan bread and tomato soup).

# 4. NITROGEN FIXERS — Native/Adapted to Tuscany

## 4.1 Trees

### Italian Alder (Alnus cordata) — *Ontano Napoletano / Ontano Cordato*
- **Native to:** Southern Apennines and Corsica.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (actinorhizal — Frankia symbiosis).
- **Height:** Up to 25m. Medium-sized tree.
- **Best for:** Gully stabilisation, riparian areas, windbreaks, nurse tree for fruit trees.
- **Soil:** Tolerates heavy clay, poor soils, moist conditions.
- **Spacing:** 5m × 5m (or 3m × 3m as nurse tree).
- **Frost risk:** Very low. Hardy to -20°C.
- **Value:** Fast-growing, provides biomass, nitrogen-enriched leaf litter.

### Carob (Ceratonia siliqua) — *Carrubo*
|- **Native to:** Mediterranean, including Italy (more common in the south).
|- **N-fixing:** **No** — carob is NOT an N-fixer. Belongs to Fabaceae but does not form N-fixing nodules. Excellent companion for dry agroforestry nonetheless.
|- **Height:** 8–15m. Evergreen.
|- **Best for:** Silvopasture, low-input systems, southern exposures.
|- **Soil:** Tolerates poor, dry, calcareous soils.
|- **Spacing:** 8m × 8m.
|- **Frost risk:** **HIGH.** Hardy to -5°C only. At 300m, this is the riskiest tree. Only plant in the warmest, most sheltered positions.
|- **Value:** Edible pods (carob flour, chocolate substitute), animal feed, drought-tolerant evergreen structure.

### Common Alder (Alnus glutinosa) — *Ontano Nero*
- **Native to:** Italy, including Tuscany.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (actinorhizal).
- **Best for:** Wet areas, stream banks.
- **Spacing:** 5m × 5m.

## 4.3 Annuals & Cover Crops

### Common Vetch (Vicia sativa) — *Veccia Comune*
- **Native to:** Mediterranean, including Italy.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
- **Use:** Winter cover crop, green manure, forage.
- **Sowing:** September–October (autumn), February–March (spring).

- **Native to:** Eurasia, naturalised in Italy.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
- **Use:** Winter cover crop, green manure, bee forage.
- **Sowing:** September–October.
- **Notes:** Beautiful red flowers. Also called "Italian clover" in some regions. Excellent for Mediterranean climate.

### Alfalfa / Lucerne (Medicago sativa) — *Erba Medica*
- **Cultivated in Italy** for millennia. Widely used in Tuscany.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
- **Use:** Forage, green manure, deep soil aeration (taproot).
- **Sowing:** March–April or September.
- **Notes:** Perennial (3–5 years). Deep taproot breaks up compacted soil. Excellent for orchard understory.

### White Lupin (Lupinus albus) — *Lupino Bianco*
- **Native to:** Mediterranean, including Italy. First domesticated by ancient Greeks ±4,000 years ago.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
- **Use:** Cover crop, green manure, seed crop (for food/feed).
- **Sowing:** October–November (autumn).
- **Notes:** Traditional Italian crop. Seeds require soaking to remove alkaloids before eating. Excellent for poor, sandy, acidic soils.

### Fava Bean (Vicia faba) — *Fava*
- **Native to:** Mediterranean, including Italy. One of the world's oldest cultivated crops.
- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
- **Tuscan varieties:** **Fava di Santa Rosa** (Lazio), **Fava Cottora** (Tuscany), **Fava di Fratte Rosa**.
- **Use:** Green manure, cover crop, food (fresh or dried).
- **Sowing:** October–November (autumn), February–March (spring).
- **Harvest:** April–May for fresh pods (baccelli), June–July for dried beans.
- **Frost tolerance:** Very high. Hardy to -10°C. Autumn-sown fava overwinters well at 300m.
- **Tuscan culinary use:** Baccelli (fresh fava beans) with pecorino as a spring antipasto. Zuppa di fave (fava bean soup).

### Squarrose Clover / Subterranean Clover (Trifolium subterraneum) — *Trifoglio Sotterraneo*
- **Naturalised in Italy, including Tuscany.**
- **N-fixing:** Yes.
- **Use:** Self-seeding annual cover crop. Perfect for orchard understory.
- **Sowing:** September–October.
- **Notes:** Excellent for dry Mediterranean summers. Self-reseeds. Low-growing.

# 6. DESIGN NOTES FOR THIS SITE

### Site Characteristics
- **1 hectare**, 300m altitude, hilly terrain
- **USDA Zone 8b–9a** (min temp -9.4°C to -6.7°C)
- **Mediterranean climate (Csa)** — hot, dry summers; mild, wet winters
- **Silty-sandy soil**, fertile, good water availability

### Key Frost Risk Considerations at 300m
- **Highest risk:** Almond (Feb–March bloom), apricot (Feb–March)
- **High risk:** Cherry (March bloom), peach (March)
- **Moderate risk:** Apple, pear, plum (March–April)
- **Low risk:** Chestnut (June bloom), persimmon (May growth), fig, pomegranate
- **Safe:** All berry bushes, hedgerow species, nitrogen fixers, and winter vegetables

### Suggested Species Distribution
- **Upper slopes (south-facing):** Olive grove (Frantoio/Leccino/Moraiolo) with understory of Spanish broom, vetch, crimson clover for N-fixation.
- **Mid-slope (orchard zone):** Mixed fruit trees (apple, pear, plum, cherry, fig, persimmon) with hazelnut and chestnut on the cooler edges. Understory: alfalfa, subterranean clover.
- **Lower slopes (water access):** Vegetable garden with annual vegetables, berries, and moisture-loving Italian alder, sea buckthorn along drainage lines.
- **Hedgerows (boundaries/contour):** Spanish broom, Rosa canina, elderberry, Colutea arborescens, Medicago arborea, with blackberries for the lower tier.

## 7.1 N-Fixing Trees (Expanded)

### Italian Alder (Alnus cordata) — *Ontano Napoletano / Ontano Cordato*
*Already detailed in §4.1. Expanded notes:*
|- **Strong N-fixer:** Excellent nurse tree. Improves soil fertility with low competition. Actinorhizal (Frankia symbiosis).
|- **Height:** Up to 20–25m.
|- **Native range:** Corsica, Calabria (southern Italy).
|- **Best for:** Northern Italy, higher elevations, wetter sites, canals, lowlands, riverbanks. Reclamation and agroforestry.
|- **Suited for 300m Tuscany:** Yes — excellent for riparian areas, gully stabilisation, and as nurse tree for fruit trees.

### Common Alder (Alnus glutinosa) — *Ontano Nero*
*Already detailed in §4.1. Expanded notes:*
|- **Native to:** Italy, including Tuscany.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (actinorhizal).
|- **Best for:** Cooler, poorer sites, wet areas, stream banks.
|- **Height:** Up to 25m.
|- **Spacing:** 5m × 5m.
|- **Frost risk:** Very low. Hardy to -25°C.

### Grey Alder (Alnus incana) — *Ontano Bianco*
|- **Native to:** Northern/Central Italy, including the Tuscan Apennines at higher elevations.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (actinorhizal — Frankia symbiosis).
|- **Height:** 15–20m.
|- **Best for:** Pioneer species, degraded sites, cooler climates.
|- **Useful for:** Timber, biomass, reclamation of poor soils. Fast-growing but shorter-lived than common alder.
|- **Frost risk:** Very low. Hardy to -30°C.
|- **Spacing:** 4m × 5m.
|- **Notes:** Less suited to dry Mediterranean conditions than Italian alder. Best for the coolest, wettest parts of the property.

## 7.2 N-Fixing Shrubs (New Entries)

### Stinking Bean Trefoil (Anagyris foetida) — *Legno Puzzo / Anagiride*
|- **Type:** Multi-stemmed shrub.
|- **Height:** Up to ~5m.
|- **Native/naturalised in:** Italy, including Sardinia, Sicily, and some southern mainland areas.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Sinorhizobium symbiosis).
|- **Forage value:** Palatable to livestock.
|- **Drought tolerance:** High. Salt-tolerant.
|- **Frost risk:** Moderate. Hardy to -8°C. At 300m, may need sheltered position.
|- **Notes:** Fast-growing. Can be invasive — manage carefully. Flowers and leaves have an unpleasant smell when crushed (hence "fetid"). Excellent pioneer species for degraded Mediterranean sites.

### Scotch Broom (Cytisus scoparius) — *Ginestra dei Carbonai*
|- **Type:** Shrub.
|- **Height:** Up to ~1.8m.
|- **Native to:** Mediterranean, including Italy. Many forms native to Tuscany.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae). Excellent N-fixer.
|- **Drought tolerance:** Ultra drought-tolerant.
|- **Best for:** Pioneer species for degraded, fire-damaged land. Rocky, dry sites, roadsides, open woodland edges.
|- **Frost risk:** Low. Hardy to -15°C.
|- **Spacing:** 1m × 1.5m.
|- **Notes:** Bright yellow flowers. Traditional use for broom-making. Excellent bee forage.

### Etna Broom (Genista aetnensis) — *Ginestra dell'Etna*
|- **Type:** Shrub to small tree.
|- **Height:** 1–4m.
|- **Native to:** Sicily (Mount Etna) and Sardinia.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Best for:** Pioneer on harsh volcanic soils (e.g., Vesuvius, Etna). Forms "fertility islands" under canopy — concentrated N-rich litter improves soil beneath.
|- **Drought tolerance:** Very high.
|- **Frost risk:** Moderate. Hardy to -8°C.
|- **Notes:** Remarkable adaptation to extreme Mediterranean conditions. Near-leafless for most of the year, photosynthesising through green stems. Golden-yellow flowers. Excellent for the most challenging sites.

### Spanish Broom (Spartium junceum) — *Ginestra di Spagna*
*Already detailed in §2.12 and §4.2. Expanded notes:*
|- **Height:** Variable, 0.5–2m.
|- **Native to:** Italy, including Tuscany. Abundant in garrique (Mediterranean scrub).
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae). High N-fixation rate.
|- **Best for:** Garrique habitats, slope stabilisation, hedgerow, bee forage.
|- **Frost risk:** Low. Hardy to -10°C.
|- **Notes:** One of the best N-fixing shrubs for Tuscan conditions. Adaptable to the driest, poorest soils.

### Tree Medic (Medicago arborea) — *Medicago Arborea / Erba Medica Arborea*
*Already detailed in §2.12 and §4.2. Expanded notes:*
|- **Height:** 1–3m.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Drought tolerance:** High.
|- **Forage value:** Excellent for livestock and wildlife.
|- **Frost risk:** Moderate. Hardy to -8°C. At 300m, plant in sheltered position.
|- **Notes:** Evergreen in mild winters. Yellow flowers. Excellent bee forage.

### Hairy Thorny Broom (Calicotome villosa) — *Calicotome / Ginestra Spinosa*
|- **Type:** Spiny shrub.
|- **Height:** 1–2m.
|- **Native to:** Mediterranean basin, including Italy (coastal/southern regions).
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Drought tolerance:** Very high.
|- **Frost risk:** Moderate. Hardy to -8°C. At 300m, best in warmest microclimate.
|- **Notes:** Very spiny — excellent for impenetrable hedgerows (barrier planting). Yellow flowers. Tolerates the poorest, driest soils. Good for wildlife habitat (nesting sites for birds).

## 7.3 Cover Crops / Green Manures (New Entries)

### White Clover (Trifolium repens) — *Trifoglio Bianco / Ladino*
|- **Type:** Perennial.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae). Fixes 50–100 kg N/ha/year.
|- **Use:** Living mulch, orchard/vineyard understory, lawns, pasture.
|- **Sowing:** March–April or September–October.
|- **Notes:** Low-growing, not competitive with tree crops. Common in Italian orchards and vineyards. Ladino clover is a large-leaved type widely used in Italy. Tolerates close mowing.

### Crimson Clover (Trifolium incarnatum) — *Trifoglio Incarnato / Trifoglio Violetto*
*Already detailed in §4.3. Expanded notes:*
|- **Type:** Annual, self-reseeding.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes.
|- **Use:** Winter cover crop, green manure, bee forage.
|- **Sowing:** September–October.
|- **Notes:** Beautiful red flowers. Also called "Italian clover" in some regions. Used in southern Italian vineyards and orchards. Self-reseeds if allowed to set seed.

### Subterranean Clover (Trifolium subterraneum) — *Trifoglio Sotterraneo*
*Already detailed in §4.3. Expanded notes:*
|- **Type:** Annual, self-seeding.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes.
|- **Use:** Self-seeding Mediterranean living mulch.
|- **Sowing:** September–October.
|- **Notes:** Excellent for dry Mediterranean conditions. Self-seeds underground (buries its own seed heads). Low-growing, non-competitive. Perfect for orchard understory in Tuscany.

### Berseem Clover (Trifolium alexandrinum) — *Trifoglio Alessandrino*
|- **Type:** Annual.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae). Strong N-fixer.
|- **Use:** Traditional southern Italian green manure. Green manure, forage.
|- **Sowing:** September–October (autumn), February–March (spring).
|- **Notes:** Fast-growing, produces abundant biomass. Strong residual fertility — leaves significant N for following crop. Tolerates slightly alkaline soils. Widely used in central and southern Italy.

### Common Vetch (Vicia sativa) — *Veccia Comune*
*Already detailed in §4.3. Expanded notes:*
|- **Type:** Annual.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes.
|- **Use:** Classic green manure, often mixed with oats or rye for biomass.
|- **Sowing:** September–October (autumn), February–March (spring).
|- **Notes:** Excellent for building soil nitrogen over winter. Killed by frost or mowing in spring. Mix with cereals for better biomass and weed suppression.

### Hairy Vetch (Vicia villosa) — *Veccia Pelosa / Veccia Vellutata*
|- **Type:** Biennial / winter annual.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Drought tolerance:** Moderate-high. More drought-tolerant than common vetch.
|- **Use:** Winter cover crop, green manure. Common in Sicilian and southern Italian systems.
|- **Sowing:** September–October.
|- **Notes:** Hairy stems and leaves. More cold-tolerant and drought-tolerant than common vetch. Excellent for dry Mediterranean hillsides. Can be used as living mulch under tree crops.

### Field Bean / Faba (Vicia faba) — *Fava*
*Already detailed in §4.3. Expanded notes:*
|- **Type:** Annual.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae). Strong N-fixer.
|- **Use:** Traditional rotation crop. Green manure, food (fresh or dried).
|- **Sowing:** October–November (autumn), February–March (spring).
|- **Frost tolerance:** Very high. Hardy to -10°C.
|- **Notes:** One of the best N-fixing cover crops for Mediterranean climate. Deep root system breaks up compacted soil. Autumn-sown fava provides the most biomass and N-fixation.

### Sulla (Hedysarum coronarium) — *Sulla / Ginestra Dolce*
|- **Type:** Biennial to short-lived perennial.
|- **Native to:** Italy and Mediterranean. Native Italian species.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Use:** Forage, green manure, bee forage.
|- **Sowing:** September–October or February–March.
|- **Notes:** Proven in Sardinian agroforestry trials. Rapid establishment. Very drought-tolerant once established. Excellent bee forage (red flowers). Deep taproot. Adapted to calcareous, alkaline soils. Highly palatable to livestock.

### Bird's-foot Trefoil (Lotus corniculatus) — *Ginestrino / Cornetta*
|- **Type:** Perennial.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Use:** N-fixing ground cover, bee forage.
|- **Sowing:** March–April or September.
|- **Notes:** Outstanding for bees. Drought-tolerant. Low-growing, non-competitive. Tolerates poor, shallow soils. Yellow flowers. Contains tannins — beneficial for livestock health (reduces bloat).

### Burr Medic (Medicago polymorpha) — *Medicago Polimorfa / Erba Medica Ruderale*
|- **Type:** Annual, self-reseeding.
|- **N-fixing:** Yes (Fabaceae).
|- **Use:** Self-reseeding ground cover for dryland.
|- **Sowing:** September–October.
|- **Notes:** Excellent for dry Mediterranean conditions. Self-seeds readily. Low-growing, works well as orchard understory. Tolerates poor soils, drought. Burr seed heads can be a nuisance — choose spineless cultivars for high-traffic areas.

### Phacelia (Phacelia tanacetifolia) — *Facelia*
|- **Type:** Annual, fast-growing.
|- **N-fixing:** **No** — phacelia is NOT an N-fixer. Included as a top companion cover crop.
|- **Use:** Fast-growing cover crop, green manure, top pollinator plant.
|- **Sowing:** March–April or September.
|- **Notes:** Outstanding bee forage — one of the best pollinator plants available. Rapid biomass production (2–3 months). Suppresses weeds effectively. Purple-blue flowers. Not a legume, but excellent in cover crop mixes alongside clovers and vetches.

### Carob (Ceratonia siliqua) — *Carrubo*
|- **N-fixing:** **No** — carob is NOT an N-fixer. Belongs to Fabaceae but does not form N-fixing nodules.
|- **Edible pods:** Used for carob flour (chocolate substitute), animal feed, syrup.
|- **Drought tolerance:** Excellent. Ideal for dry, calcareous soils.
|- **Frost risk:** **HIGH.** Hardy to -5°C only. At 300m, only in warmest, most sheltered positions.
|- **Companion value:** Excellent evergreen companion for dry agroforestry. Provides structure, shade, and edible pods. Not a nitrogen source but a valuable multi-functional tree.
